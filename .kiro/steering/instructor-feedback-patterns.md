# Instructor Feedback Patterns — ENGL 1102-01 (English Composition 2)

This file distills the instructor's written feedback across Units 1–7 of ENGL 1102-01 for
both students in the household. It is name-agnostic and identical in both repositories, so it
applies to any research-paper, report, or discussion work for this course. Every item below
traces to real feedback on work that otherwise scored 100/100, so treat each one as a
refinement that is likely to be raised again unless it is handled in the first draft.

## 1. Define how every variable is measured

- Never leave a key term ("practical speed," "adaptability," "technical grounding,"
  "conceptual understanding," "self-rated learning") undefined. State the instrument, the
  scale, who scores it, and when.
- When a rubric is involved, say how it is built (by whom, before data collection), how it is
  piloted, and how consistency is checked (two raters unaware of group, agreement reported,
  disagreements resolved by discussion). Give the rubric criteria and score range.
- Use one common form of any test for all participants so difficulty is the same for every
  group, and say so.
- Self-reports alone are not enough evidence of skill; pair them with performance evidence.

## 2. Handle selection, prior experience, and causation explicitly

- If participants choose their own group (AI use, training route), say the comparison is
  observational and nonequivalent, and describe results as associations, never as proof of
  cause.
- Name the likely confounds (prior experience, motivation, access to resources, family or
  financial background, type of workplace or school) and say how each will be measured at
  baseline and used as a control.
- Explain the rationale for any threshold used to define a group (for example, "at least
  three assignments"), and say what happens to people who fall between groups.
- Give a classification rule for people who fit two groups (for example, a cook with both a
  culinary program and substantial work experience): a separate mixed group analyzed
  exploratorily is acceptable.
- Treat a sample size as "a starting point," not a final number. Adequacy depends on the
  expected difference, variability, and recruiting feasibility; a power calculation using pilot
  data sets the final target.

## 3. Be precise about what each source does and does not show

- Before stating that "no study does X" (for example, no follow-up beyond the course),
  verify it against each study's full text, not only abstracts. When a full text cannot be
  accessed (some publisher sites block downloads), say in the paper that only the abstract was
  read and limit the claim accordingly.
- Do not treat a related outcome as direct evidence of the variable of interest
  (satisfaction is not performance; job fit is not skill; perceptions are not ability).
- Say what each source measured and found, and what it cannot establish; a source that
  describes how skill is learned does not compare two routes.
- Compare methodologies (ethnography, quasi-experiment, survey, meta-analysis,
  comparison study) and explain how each affects the strength of the evidence.
- Keep the required source set closed for review sections. Any extra source belongs only in
  background or methodology, with its role labeled.

## 4. Mixed-methods details

- Say how interview participants are selected (purposive, for contrast: high course grade but
  low later score; fastest and slowest; heavy and light users), not just how many.
- Say how interview data will be analyzed alongside the numbers (coding, a joint table of each
  interviewee's scores beside their themes).
- Say how both groups will complete comparable tasks under the same conditions (same room,
  equipment, ingredients or materials, instructions, order randomized, one planned disruption
  at the same point).

## 5. Interpreting data and making recommendations (reports, charts)

- Qualify any interpretation the data cannot establish directly. Example: travel time may
  include commuting, but a chart does not show it is all for class or work.
- A recommendation must follow from the evidence. Do not recommend cutting an activity
  (such as leisure) simply because it is large. Prefer advice to review individual schedules
  while protecting essentials such as sleep.
- The absence of inferential statistics in a displayed summary limits the conclusions that can
  be drawn from that display; it does not, by itself, show the underlying survey cannot
  support population-level estimates.

## 6. Abstracts and structure

- Keep the abstract self-contained. Put any introductory remarks about writing guidelines and
  any closing reflection on the drafting process outside the abstract (or omit them).
- An abstract is about 150–250 words and carries no citations.

## 7. Register and style

- Reduce conversational phrasing (no rhetorical-question openers) and tighten long sentences.
- For the final compiled paper, follow the assigned Unit 8 readings: avoid contractions
  (Hall & Wallace, 2018, Section 5.1) and check the frequent APA errors listed in Serdikoff
  (2021, p. 738): numerals for 10 and above, hyphenated compound modifiers, a serial comma,
  abbreviations spelled out at first use, "&" only inside parentheses and reference lists.
- Cite article-number journals as "Article N", not as a page number (for example, MDPI
  *Sustainability* 12(5), Article 1951).

## 8. Final compiled paper (Unit 8) checklist

- Components: abstract, introduction, background, problem statement, objectives and
  questions, literature review, methodology, discussion of results, conclusion, references.
- When no study has been run, the "discussion of results" covers what the existing evidence
  shows, what each possible outcome would mean, implications, limitations, and future work
  (Serdikoff, 2021, p. 766). Never invent data.
- Show visible editing from earlier units (a short revision note naming concrete changes) and
  use the assigned Unit 8 readings (Serdikoff Sections 17, 58, 59; Weaver Section 2.4; Hall &
  Wallace Sections 4.7 and 5.1–5.4).
- Word range is 1,000–3,500 excluding the title and reference list. Keep a buffer below 3,500.
