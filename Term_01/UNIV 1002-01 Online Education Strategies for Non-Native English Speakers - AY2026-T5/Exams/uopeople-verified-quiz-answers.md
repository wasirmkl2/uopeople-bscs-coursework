### Netiquette question logic

The wrong options in netiquette questions are consistently the **overclaims**. Netiquette does **not**:

- replicate or replace nonverbal cues (it compensates for their absence)
- remove the need for instructor moderation
- guarantee agreement among participants
- mean online discussions have no rules, require less effort, or are inherently more focused

Correct answers stay modest: netiquette keeps discussion **polite, focused, and productive**, and the defining feature of online discussion is the **absence of visual and auditory cues**. This matches the Netiquette transcript's point that tone of voice cannot be heard, so sarcasm should be avoided.

**Emoticons are a trap, not a fix.** A statement pairing "it's easy to miscommunicate online" (true) with "using emoticons is a strategy to improve communication" (this course treats as false) is **False overall** — CONFIRMED. This course's specific sources (CSU Netiquette, UoPeople Do's/Don'ts) instruct avoiding emoticons/emojis in academic writing, unlike generic informal netiquette guides that endorse them. Don't default to general internet advice on emoticons for this course.

### Ch. 5.3 Taking Notes — the benefits list

The text names these note-taking benefits: notes **organize your ideas** and help you make meaning; they help you **stay focused on the question at hand**; strong notes **build on prior knowledge**, help you **discuss trends or patterns**, and **direct you toward areas needing further research or reading**. Well-written notes help you organize thoughts, **enhance memory**, **participate in class discussion**, and **prepare for exams**.

**Reducing the amount of time you spend reading is NOT a listed benefit** — that is what skimming/surveying does. This is the answer to "which is *not* a benefit."

Also from 5.3:
- Do **not** transcribe every word; listen for main ideas vs. supporting detail.
- "Think of all notes as **potential study guides**."
- Without active engagement after note-taking, students **forget 60-75 percent within two days** — the **Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve**. Beware the reversed distractor claiming students *remember* 60-75 percent for two days or more; that is **false**.
- **Review timing: "make every effort to review your notes within 24 hours of first taking them."** Longer risks forgetting; the Cornell section specifies **preferably within eight hours, no more than twenty-four**. So **24 hours**, not 48.
- Annotation = highlighting, underlining, defining terms, margin questions, circling key terms. Mantra: **"less is more."** Always read the selection **before** highlighting. Do not over-mark.

### Ch. 5.2 Effective Reading Strategies

- **SQ3R** = **Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review** (Francis Pleasant Robinson, *Effective Study*, 1961).
- In the **Read** step: "When you finish reading the selection, stop to make notes. Answer the questions by **writing a note in the margin or other white space** of the text." You may also *carefully* underline or highlight — but **"Don't over-mark."** So "underline or highlight **as much material as possible**" is **wrong**.
- Active reading takes **at least twice as long** as passive reading. A full course load may require **10-15 hours of reading per week**.
- **Recursive reading** — reading for college is rereading; a circular, not linear, process. Six elements: prior knowledge, asking questions, inferring/implying, learning vocabulary, evaluating, applying.
- Read assigned material **before** the lecture, not after.
- **Role of assigned readings:** the text says your instructor "may want you to have a **general background** on a topic before you dive into that subject in class, so that you know the history of a topic, can start thinking about it, and can engage in a class discussion with more than a passing knowledge of the issue." This supports **"they provide essential background knowledge and context for assignments."**
- On reading's importance, 5.2 cites Truman ("Not all readers are leaders, but all leaders are readers"), Jefferson, Douglass and Martin — so any claim that weak reading won't affect future success is **False**.
- Beyond a standard internet search, **try the library article database** to research a topic or author — confirms the Unit 4 quiz item.

### Ch. 4.4 Managing Change and the Unexpected — planning as a continuous process

Direct support for the "continuous process" fill-in-the-blank: the text states that decision-making and **planning are continuous processes** — active individuals are always engaged in **decision-making, setting new plans, and revising old plans**. It adds that this often happens simply because new information shifts our plans, and that **planning, like learning, is an ongoing lifetime process**. So the blank is **Planning**.

Also from 4.4:
- Change comes from **internal factors** (within your control — indecision, changed mind, poor fit with values) or **external factors** (harder to plan for — finances, health, family, not being admitted, poor course performance).
- Questions to ask when plans change: What can I control? Do I need to reconsider my **values**? My **goals**? My **plans**? What resources/people can help?
- **Seeking help is a strength, not a weakness.** Others can help you plan and hold you accountable, **but only you set your values**.
- A **mentor** is an experienced person guiding a **mentee**; ideally knowledgeable in your desired field and beyond entry level.

Ch. 4 learning objective states it outright: "Recognize **decision-making and planning as continuous processes**, especially in response to unexpected change."

### Ch. 4.2 Planning Your Degree Path — credit thresholds

- **Associate's degree: minimum 60 credits**, roughly 20 courses.
- **Bachelor's degree: minimum 120 credits**, roughly 40 courses. A transferred associate's usually covers about half.
- Master's: **30-60 credits**. Doctorate: an additional **90-120 credits** beyond the bachelor's.
- **Graduation is not credits alone:** "although a minimum number of credits must be completed ... total credits completed is **not the only consideration** for graduation—you must take your credits or courses in **particular subjects** indicated by your college." So the correct graduation-requirement answer is always **credits in designated subject areas**, never "any combination" or "automatic once enough credits."
- Bachelor's structure: **general education (core curriculum), major courses, electives**, plus optional minor. Gen ed is **more than half** of a bachelor's. Major ≈ 10-12 courses / 30 credits; minor ≈ 5-8 courses. Majors are typically declared by **30 credits**.

## Key facts from video transcripts in this repo

### Netiquette (Cambrian College)
Be formal with professors when unsure ("Professor", not a first name); avoid slang (hey, sup, cheers). Give first **and** last name, your course, and a full explanation. Think before Reply All. Use please and thank you when requesting extensions. Use one consistent email address. In forums: share differing views honestly but constructively; **avoid sarcasm** because tone of voice is invisible; be mindful that classmates carry lived trauma. Don't post while angry — write it, **sleep on it, reread, edit, then post**. No ALL CAPS (reads as screaming); same for excess exclamation marks.

### Reading Efficiently (RMIT)
You are not required to read everything word-for-word. Three techniques:
- **Survey** — title, cover blurb, contents page, index, chapter headings
- **Skim** — main ideas only
- **Scan** — keywords and specific detail

Afterward, detailed reading is easier because you know where the key ideas are.

### Why People Fall for Misinformation (Isaac, 2020, TED-Ed)
David Hänig, **1901**, Leipzig University dissertation (in German) → the **tongue/taste map**, a **misconception**: widely believed but largely incorrect. Hänig did find small variations in sensitivity (sweet at tip, bitter at back, salty, sour along mid-sides) but noted every taste is perceptible **across the whole tongue**.
- **Disinformation** = false information **intentionally** designed to mislead
- **Misinformation** = false/misleading information from **unintentional** inaccuracy

Three causes of the spread: (1) language barrier creating a "game of telephone"; (2) an oversimplified 1912 newspaper illustration cited repeatedly **without credit or nuance**, which reached textbooks; (3) **narrative simplicity** — we prefer clean stories over messy science. **Umami** (savory) is now the fifth basic taste; fatty, alkaline, metallic, water-like are still debated. Lesson: maintain healthy skepticism toward convenient charts and surprising anecdotes.

### Why Feedback is Important (Sam Elsey, TEDx)
Opens with losing a tennis match at 11, ignoring his trainer, and losing the trainer's confidence. Definition of feedback: **my action → your reaction → my observation of and adaptation to that reaction.** Types: visual, auditive, sensory, and the focus of the talk, **social feedback**.

**The four C's of good feedback:**
1. **Contextual** — right time, when the person is most receptive
2. **Constructive** — not "this is bad"; lead with a compliment, then what to improve
3. **Concise** — short, five to ten phrases max
4. **Concrete** — a specific example of what to improve

**Three criteria for judging feedback received:** **source** (who), **motive** (with what intention), **credibility / "kredos"** (with what qualifications). If they check out, use it; if not, consider disregarding it. Metaphors: feedback is a **gift** (wrap it, give it at the right time and manner) and a **seed** planted in someone's mind. "I am not my potential" — the people around you are your potential.

### Growth Mindset: The Power of Yet (Dweck, 2021)
A Chicago high school graded failures **"Not Yet"** instead of failing — a failing grade says "I'm nothing, I'm nowhere," while "not yet" puts you on a **learning curve** with a path to the future.

Study: 10-year-olds given problems slightly too hard.
- **Growth mindset** — "I love a challenge," believe abilities can be developed
- **Fixed mindset** — reacted as if tragic/catastrophic; **the "tyranny of now"** instead of the power of yet

Fixed-mindset consequences: said they'd **cheat** next time rather than study more; sought out someone who did **worse** to feel better; ran from difficulty. **EEG/brain-activity evidence:** fixed-mindset students show hardly any activity when confronting an error (they disengage); growth-mindset students engage deeply, process the error, learn, correct it — "their brain is on fire with yet."

Interventions: **praise the process** — effort, strategies, focus, perseverance, improvement — **not intelligence or talent** (praising intelligence "has failed"). A math game rewarding effort/strategy/progress produced more effort and perseverance than one rewarding right answers. Teaching that pushing out of your comfort zone forms **new, stronger neural connections** reversed declining grades during a hard school transition.

Equity results: a **Harlem kindergarten** class reached the **95th percentile** on the National Achievement Test in one year (many couldn't hold a pencil on arrival); **fourth graders on a Native American reservation** went from the **bottom to the top of their district**, which included affluent Seattle areas — "the native kids outdid the Microsoft kids." Effort and difficulty were reframed from "I'm dumb" to "my neurons are making stronger connections."

### In-text Citations Made Easy (Chelsea Seaburn, APA 7)
Purpose: distinguish your ideas from others'; failure to credit is **plagiarism** — and intent is irrelevant, you're equally liable. Penalties: lost points, failing grades, possibly failing the course. An in-text citation is a **shortened** citation pointing to the full reference list entry.

Always **author–date** (author's last name + year). **Source locators** are required for **direct quotes**, optional for paraphrases unless they'd significantly help the reader: page number (books), **timestamp** (video/audio), **slide number** (PowerPoint), paragraph number or section heading (webpages).

Two formats:
- **Parenthetical** — (Author, 2020); most common; usually end of sentence, comma + one space between elements
- **Narrative** — Author (2020) woven into the sentence; locator goes at the end

Scenarios:
- **2 authors** — **&** in parenthetical, spelled-out **and** in narrative
- **3+ authors** — first author + **et al.** (period after "al" before the comma)
- **Group author** — write the full name; if abbreviable, put the abbreviation in **brackets** on first use, then use the abbreviation afterward (e.g., World Health Organization [WHO])
- **No author** — use a **shortened title**; italicize if italicized in the reference list, otherwise use **double quotation marks**
- **No date** — **n.d.**
- **Multiple sources at once** — separate with **semicolons** inside one set of parentheses; narrative uses commas
- **Same author + same year, different works** — append lowercase letters (2020a, 2020b)

Quotes:
- **Direct quote** — **fewer than 40 words**; in double quotation marks; locator required; sentence punctuation outside the closing parenthesis
- **Block quote** — **40 or more words**; starts on its own line, indented **one half inch**, double-spaced, **no quotation marks**, and the citation goes **after** the closing punctuation

Advice: use direct quotes sparingly — one or two per paper at most, ideally none. Instructors prefer your own paraphrasing and critical thinking.

### Correctly Use and Reference ChatGPT (Chelsea Seaburn, APA 7)
APA has official guidance. AI may **facilitate research and generate limited text** — it is **not** a green light to write your paper. **Your professor's instructions override this** if they differ.

Because AI gives a different response to the same prompt, you must include **the prompt you used and the exact generated text verbatim**, structured like a **direct quote**, and keep the insert **short**. For a long response, put the full text in an **appendix** and note "see Appendix A for the full transcript" in the in-text citation (mainly relevant to long manuscripts / publication).

AI output is neither a **retrievable source** (books, journals, web pages) nor a **personal communication** (emails, lectures, slides). APA classifies it as an **algorithm's output**. So the "author" is the **company that built the model**, not a person.

Reference template — Company. (Year of version). *Name of AI* (Version) [Descriptor]. URL

Example: OpenAI. (2023). *ChatGPT* (Mar 14 version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

In-text: **(OpenAI, 2023)** parenthetical, or **OpenAI (2023)** narrative. Note the title is **italicized**, the version goes in **parentheses**, and the descriptor goes in **square brackets**. **Image generators** follow a slightly different variation.
