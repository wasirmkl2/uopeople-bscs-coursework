## Part 1: Stress Management

If stress goes unmanaged, it doesn't just disappear on its own, it builds up over time and starts affecting concentration, sleep, and even physical health (Baldwin et al., 2020). That's exactly why stress management matters. Day-to-day pressure from schoolwork doesn't really shake me, since I try not to dwell on problems and lean toward **optimism** when something goes wrong. Public speaking is a different story, though; that's where stress actually gets to me. My nerves take over the moment I have to give a presentation, and that kind of situational stress is much harder to shake off than ordinary daily pressure.

One habit I already lean on is talking through my stress with my wife. Putting it into words and hearing her perspective back almost always brings my stress level down, so it is probably the most effective tool I have. Music is a close second, since the right song works almost like a short meditation and lets my mind reset.

Meditation is the new practice I want to add. I have heard about it constantly, from classmates to this unit's own reading, but I have never sat down and tried it in a structured way. My plan for determining whether it works is to track how calm I feel walking into a stressful situation, like a presentation, before and after a few weeks of practice. If my nervousness in those moments drops, meditation earns a permanent spot in my toolkit. This kind of toolkit takes **self-directed** effort to build, since no one hands you a finished stress plan; you test what works and adjust it yourself, and that process builds **resilience** over time.

## Part 2: Test Anxiety

My final exam preparation will center on timed practice sessions, working through problems under the same time pressure I will face on exam day, so the real test feels familiar (Baldwin et al., 2020). A quiet, distraction-free study space matters too, since background noise pulls my focus away fast, and full concentration during preparation gives me the **capability** to actually retain what I studied.

Exam day itself is different: I am not going to study that morning. Cramming right before an exam raises my stress rather than my score, so I would rather give my brain a break and walk in with a clear head and quiet **self-regulation** instead of last-minute panic. What matters most in that moment is reminding myself that I prepared and know the material, rather than fixating on worst-case outcomes that keep test anxiety in control (Baldwin et al., 2020).

I picked these strategies because they match how I already respond to stress. Feeling prepared and in control keeps me calm, and that's exactly what timed practice paired with a quiet study space gives me before the exam even starts. By skipping morning cramming, I also protect the **flexibility** I need to stay level-headed under pressure. Compared to how I used to handle exams, studying right up until the last minute and walking in already stressed, this reflects real **progress**.
