**Subject:** Feedback Request: Mixed-Methods Study of AI Coding Assistants in CS1

Dear Professor Rajith,

I'd like your advice on a research idea from ENGL 1102. I'm still learning to program, and I worry that an AI coding assistant helps me finish code faster without helping me understand it. The studies I reviewed suggest these tools can raise immediate performance, but I haven't found one that follows students far beyond the course to see whether the advantage lasts once the tool is gone. My study would compare students in a first programming course (CS1) who used an AI assistant on at least three graded assignments with peers who did not, using an AI-free concept check one semester after the course ends.

I think a mixed-methods design fits best. It combines quantitative and qualitative approaches (Serdikoff, 2021), and my study needs both: one part to measure whether the advantage persists, another to find out why. The quantitative part would compare the groups' delayed-check scores among CS1 students I can realistically reach (National University, 2024).

Numbers only go so far, though. Serdikoff (2021) notes that quantitative research can give precise answers and general conclusions, but it shows less about what a situation is like for the people in it. Interviews would help with that. I'd talk with a few students about how they handled problems without the tool, then see whether what they describe helps explain the scores. Prather et al. (2024) used observations and interviews with CS1 students using Copilot and uncovered cognitive and metacognitive difficulties, which suggests scores alone may not reveal them.

I'd welcome your thoughts on whether interviewing a few students is realistic for a one-semester follow-up, and on how to handle students choosing for themselves whether to use AI. I can send my Unit 4 draft if that helps.

Thank you for your time.
