# Discussion Forum Unit 2: Argumentative Mode — Does Remote Work Really Enhance Productivity?

**Posted by:** S M Wasir Jayed Rafi
**Course:** ENGL 1102-01 — English Composition 2 (AY2027-T1)

The argumentative mode tries to persuade through evidence and reasoning, not emotion. Mallika (2024) describes it as writing that takes a clear position and defends it with logic, credible sources, and support, and that also has to answer whatever objection is likely to come up without pretending nobody will raise it. That last part, dealing directly with the other side, is really what separates this mode from something closer to persuasion, which leans harder on emotional appeal than on direct rebuttal.

Ask five people whether working from home makes them more productive, and you'll probably get five different answers, because the honest answer depends less on the job and more on the person doing it. A thesis is supposed to stake out a specific stance rather than dodge the question (Hall & Wallace, 2018), so here's mine: remote work does raise productivity, but only for someone who already brings the discipline the arrangement quietly requires. Take that discipline away, and the same setup that helps one person finish early ends up dragging another person behind.

For me, that discipline is what makes remote work actually work. I get to set up in my own space, work at whatever hour I focus best, and take a short break when I actually need one, without waiting for a fixed office clock to say it's allowed. None of that flexibility does anything on its own, though. It only pays off because I still hold myself to finishing tasks on time, whether or not anyone is checking. The freedom is real, but it only turns into productivity once someone decides to use it that way and not just enjoy it.

Hand that same setup to someone who procrastinates, and the story flips fast. Nobody is standing over their desk noticing an hour has slipped by on something unrelated to work, and no coworker walks past to nudge them back on track. A task that should take thirty minutes stretches into an afternoon, not because the job got harder, but because the structure that used to force action, a supervisor nearby, a fixed start time, a room where "working" was the only option, quietly disappeared. Remote work didn't cause that drift. It just removed the guardrails that had been covering for it.

Critics of remote work usually point to exactly that drift as proof the whole arrangement is flawed, arguing that without direct supervision, most people will drift toward distraction over discipline. That's a fair concern, worth taking seriously rather than brushing off, which is part of what the argumentative mode expects from a writer defending an argument (Mallika, 2024). The same logic, though, works just as well against an office. Plenty of people procrastinate in a cubicle too, scrolling a phone rather than working, just with a boss nearby instead of far away. If procrastination happens in both settings, the setting isn't really the deciding factor. The person is.

So the debate over remote work probably isn't really about location at all. It's about whether someone can hold themselves accountable when nobody else is going to do it for them. I can, and that's exactly why working from my own place, on my own schedule, with breaks I decide on myself, makes me more productive, not less. Someone without that same discipline would likely get the opposite result from the identical setup, which says less about remote work itself and more about what a person brings into it.
